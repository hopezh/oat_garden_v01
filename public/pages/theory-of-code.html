The Theory of Code: The Last Software Engineering Skill

As AI makes writing code trivial, the decisive skill is a robust mental model — a "theory" — of your system. It's what lets you verify, steer, and own AI output instead of quietly eroding your architecture.

Overview

The argument in one paragraph

As artificial intelligence makes the physical act of writing code easier, the most critical skill for a modern engineer is the development of a robust mental model — a "theory" — of the software system.

Tools like Claude can generate functional syntax rapidly, but they lack the contextual reasoning required to maintain a clean architecture, often leading to a "sloppy" codebase that erodes over time if left unchecked. Seniority is no longer defined by programming speed, but by the ability to verify, steer, and take responsibility for AI-generated output based on a deep understanding of the product's constraints. The narrative warns against "vibe coding" and against shifting the burden of quality to reviewers — asserting that human decision-making remains the only evergreen asset in a landscape of rapidly changing technology.

The core thesis

Theory bounds AI

AI's performance is bounded by the theory you bring to it — not the other way around.

Verify > generate

The complexity isn't in generating code anymore; it's having enough knowledge to verify it's right.

Evergreen skill

Building theory fast is the new meta-skill — it doesn't expire with the next model release.

The Story

The pull request that looked right

The author spent seven years learning hard skills — system design, data structures and algorithms, clean code — only to find them becoming less important to the day-to-day job. When his company began interviewing engineers, every question targeted one specific skill, and no one had it. The work was supposed to get easier; instead it became harder to find people who actually have what it takes.

On a clean, stable system built from scratch — one where features shipped fast — a new mid-level engineer joined. Excited, productive, and using Claude Code from day one, exactly as encouraged. His first pull request landed on day two. The feature worked. It was rejected completely.

What was wrong: the validation lived in the wrong layer; business logic wasn't where it was supposed to be.

  • He didn't see it — because he didn't write it.
  • Claude didn't see it — because it has no theory.
  • Neither of them had been there for the bug that motivated the working pattern in the first place.

He wasn't a bad engineer — the interview proved that. He was a fast engineer, confidently using a powerful tool exactly the way he'd been told to, while quietly eroding years of architecture faster than anyone could articulate why. In a couple of such PRs, he went from promising to annoying.

The turnaround

He stopped opening pull requests and started reading the codebase "like a foreign language he meant to become fluent in." He built his own theory of the product, then ran every AI suggestion through it before he ever opened a PR. The codebase stopped getting sloppy — and he became a valuable member of the team.

Code that looks like it belongs — but doesn't

AI generates code that looks like it belongs, but doesn't. You can't tell the difference without a theory of what your codebase is actually trying to be. That gap — between plausible and correct — is exactly what a mental model exists to close.

AI Reality

Powerful at finding, weak at fixing

People say AI is good at finding bugs — and it is. The author's workflow is to launch Claude with a debug prompt while, in parallel, his own mental model scouts for the location. But finding is not fixing.

AI debugging performance in a mature codebase

It locates the problem fast — but the proposed fix is usually a local patch for the symptom.

Bug location found — very fast~95%
Proposed fix actually acceptable~20%

A 75-point gap between locating a bug and actually fixing its root cause.

Bugs are violated assumptions, not syntax errors

Bugs in mature codebases aren't local syntax errors — they are violated assumptions from the design process. You need theory to decode the AI's finding and fix the actual cause. AI can find a location, but without theory you're making a blind decision about how to fix it.

AI is powerful, but it's not a magic wand. A location without a theory is just a more precise place to guess.
The Theory Edge

A good prompt is compressed theory

The current narrative gets it backwards: it claims AI replaces theory-building with CLAUDE.md files. In reality, AI's performance is bounded by the theory you bring to it.

  • Dumping API docs into a prompt will not make Claude know your project.
  • One sentence explaining the main constraints and invariants of the target system will steer it in the right direction — and that's mostly what the author does now.
  • A good prompt is compressed theory: very specific, very accurate to the current task and module.

Seniority, redefined

Senior engineers outperform juniors with AI not because they use magic words, but because stronger mental models let them act with control and precision — and with fewer tokens.

◷ The old definition
  • Programming speed
  • Memorized algorithms
  • Lines of code shipped
  • Knowing the latest tool
✓ The new definition
  • Verify — confirm output is actually right
  • Redirect — catch decisions that violate the design
  • Decline — reject suggestions that don't fit
  • Steer — guide with precision, fewer tokens

Visible business value

In a meeting with stakeholders, when someone asks "Can we do X?" or "What if we do Y?", your ability to answer immediately is what makes you visibly valuable to the business.

If the only thing that knows your codebase is AI, then nobody actually knows your codebase. You either built the theory or you didn't.
The Mandate

The mistake worth firing over

Juniors, mid-levels, and even seniors leaning on AI make this mistake almost daily: they transfer the responsibility for their messy code onto the reviewer. The tempting workflow —

get the task → spin up Claude → vibe-code the tests → glance at the result → submit

— means your teammates build the theory while you skim. So what are you actually being paid for?

"As a reviewer I do not care what the AI said. AI output is noise. I want to see contributors thinking." What belongs in a PR isn't two pages of AI-generated docs nobody reads — it's the theory a real engineer built while working on it. — Mitchell Hashimoto, maintainer of Ghostty Terminal (and notably very pro-AI)

The author admits to the same trap six months earlier: he vibe-coded an important feature, it looked fine, and out of laziness he submitted it. His team lead called him to discuss it and had good arguments for why the design was wrong — but he couldn't defend it, because he didn't even know why he'd built it that way. He'd wasted his team's time reviewing something that wasn't ready. That was his responsibility, not theirs.

The golden rule

"I don't commit anything I couldn't explain to someone else."

What's actually hard

Debugging, code review, and meetings are all secondary. In the AI era, the real job is making decisions that serve the product long-term — and decision-making is entirely a function of theory. The speed of producing badly designed software is worth nothing.

Writing code is the easiest part of software engineering, and it's getting easier by the day. The hard parts are everything you do with that code across its entire life cycle:

Easiest · automatableWrite

Generating syntax — now trivial.

HardUnderstand

Hold an accurate model of it.

HardExtend

Add to it without breaking it.

HardGovern

Steward its quality over its life.

Building theory fast is the new meta-skill.

Don't chase shiny tools

While you learn an important skill, there's always a tab open with the latest AI announcement stressing you out: orchestrate ten agents in parallel, research the newest workflow, or stay behind. Investing heavily in something that may be obsolete with the next model release can feel foolish — and someone is always waving an easy, shiny thing in front of you, profiting from that anxiety while your foundation rots.

Isn't building an evergreen skill better than chasing one shiny tool after another? It is. When a new model or tool drops: take a look, evaluate how useful it is, then go back to your actual work — because the skill you're building isn't going anywhere. It's yours.
