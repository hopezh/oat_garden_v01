Research, Plan, Build

A digest of Matt Van Horn's two field guides on his agentic engineering workflow. The throughline: the instant you have an idea, you don't type code — you run /ce-plan to make a durable plan.md, ground it in real-time research, and build by voice across a pile of parallel terminal sessions.

Overview

From "vibe coding" to agentic engineering

TL;DR

Matt Van Horn argues that what people called "vibe coding" matured — around late 2025 — into a real, repeatable discipline he calls Agentic Engineering. The unchanging rule #1: the instant you have an idea, you don't start typing code — you run /ce-plan to produce a durable plan.md, then let agents execute it.SRC 1

The loop reduces to three words — Research → Plan → Build — powered by the Compound Engineering plugin, driven mostly by voice, run across four-to-six terminal tabs. June's biggest additions: don't read the plan (plans are for agents), make a plan for the plan for deep non-code work, be the human signal, write your own skills, and a fleet of real-life CLIs that book trips and preheat the car.SRC 1

The honest coda: this loop is genuinely addictive. Guard your relationships, and build things people actually want — even if "people" is just you.SRC 1

The thesis: the bottleneck moved. Generating syntax is cheap. The leverage is in planning well, keeping that plan as a durable artifact, and spending your scarce human attention on taste and direction instead of typing.

The Core Loop

The three-word workflow: Research, Plan, Build

Everything hangs on one small loop, and each stage maps to a command from the Compound Engineering plugin (by @kieranklaassen and @trevin).SRC 1

01 · Research /last30days

Pull what the community knows right now across Reddit, X, YouTube, HN and more — not six-month-old training data.

02 · Plan /ce-plan

Fan out research agents in parallel, then consolidate into a structured plan.md: problem, approach, files, checkbox acceptance criteria.

03 · Build /ce-work

Take the plan, break it into tasks, implement, test, and check off the acceptance criteria — optionally routed to Codex with --codex.

The plan-file rules

  • Plan first, always. The plan is the durable artifact; the code is downstream of it. New idea → /ce-plan before anything else.SRC 1
  • Brainstorm when it's fuzzy. /ce-brainstorm turns a half-formed thought into something a plan can be built on.SRC 1
  • Don't read the plan. Plans are written for agents to execute, not for you to proofread line by line — reading them is a waste of your scarce attention.SRC 1
  • Plan for the plan. For deep non-code work (his example: a book plus its transcript), first plan how you'll plan, then let the loop run.SRC 1
The two articles were themselves written this way — dictated into the terminal, rewritten on command, and reviewed in Proof.SRC 1·2
Voice & Signal

Talk to it; be the taste

Two of the most load-bearing hacks aren't tools at all — they're a change in input method and a change in mindset.

Get voice-pilled

Typing is the throttle on how much you can drive the agents. Van Horn dictates almost everything through voice input (Monologue / Wispr Flow), which lets him keep four-to-six sessions fed without his hands becoming the bottleneck — from a desk, a couch, a car, or the sideline of a soccer field.SRC 1

Be the human signal

The reframing that ties everything together: in an agentic workflow your value isn't producing keystrokes, it's supplying judgment and direction. As he puts it —

"Be the taste, let them be the hands." — Matt Van Horn, on being the human signal

That principle is what the chart below tries to make concrete: the human contributes a small slice of high-value signal; the agents do the overwhelming majority of the hands-on execution.

Remote & autonomous

The workflow doesn't need to be at the keyboard at all. A Mac mini stays on at home, reachable from anywhere, so sessions keep running while he's away.SRC 1·2

Beyond Code

The same loop leaves the editor

The biggest jump from March to June is scope. Once research → plan → build is reliable for code, the same loop runs meetings, media, knowledge, and real-world errands.SRC 1

Research first

Start non-code work with /last30days too — current community knowledge beats stale training data.

Meetings → Granola

Feed raw Granola transcripts in; a dedicated Printing Press CLI handles them (it was copy-paste, then MCP, in March).

Video → HyperFrames

Generate and edit video as part of the same agent-driven pipeline.

Notes as a knowledge base

Bear / Obsidian (with supermemory) become a queryable store the agents read from and write to.

Share via Proof

Hand a plan to non-technical collaborators in Proof instead of making them read raw Markdown.

Real-world CLIs

Printing Press + Agent Cookie run errands — booking a Disney trip, preheating the car — from the same loop.

Compounding

Two hacks turn the workflow into a flywheel: write your own skills so repeated tasks get cheaper every time, and contribute to the open source you love so the tools you depend on keep improving — which is how he ended up near the top of so many contributor lists.SRC 1

The takeaway

Neither article is really about an IDE or a single tool. It's that voice input, a durable plan.md, parallel sessions and a growing fleet of CLIs compose into one repeatable operating model — and that same model now powers code, strategy, writing, open source, and Disney trips alike.SRC 1·2

Research, plan, build. No IDE worship, no vibe-coding for its own sake — talk, produce a plan that survives, and be the signal while agents do the hands-on work. From a desk, a couch, a car, or a soccer field.
