Talk, Plan, Build

Matt Van Horn's two field guides to "Agentic Engineering" — the March origin and its 22-hack June sequel. The throughline: start every idea with a plan.md, ground it in real-time research, and build by voice across a pile of parallel terminal sessions.

🧵 2 source articles 📋 22 hacks digested 🕒 ~10 min read 🔗 @mvanhorn
Overview

From "vibe coding" to agentic engineering

TL;DR

Matt Van Horn argues that what people called "vibe coding" matured — around late 2025 — into a real, repeatable discipline he calls Agentic Engineering. The unchanging rule #1: the instant you have an idea, you don't start typing code — you run /ce-plan to produce a durable plan.md, then let agents execute it.SRC 1

The loop reduces to three words — Research → Plan → Build — powered by the Compound Engineering plugin, driven mostly by voice, run across four-to-six terminal tabs. June's biggest additions: don't read the plan (plans are for agents), make a plan for the plan for deep non-code work, be the human signal, write your own skills, and a fleet of real-life CLIs that book trips and preheat the car.SRC 1

The honest coda: this loop is genuinely addictive. Guard your relationships, and build things people actually want — even if "people" is just you.SRC 1

/ce-plan /ce-brainstorm /ce-work /last30days Compound Engineering cmux voice-first

The thesis: the bottleneck moved. Generating syntax is cheap. The leverage is in planning well, keeping that plan as a durable artifact, and spending your scarce human attention on taste and direction instead of typing.

The Core Loop

The three-word workflow: Research, Plan, Build

Everything hangs on one small loop, and each stage maps to a command from the Compound Engineering plugin (by @kieranklaassen and @trevin).SRC 1

01 · Research /last30days

Pull what the community knows right now across Reddit, X, YouTube, HN and more — not six-month-old training data.

02 · Plan /ce-plan

Fan out research agents in parallel, then consolidate into a structured plan.md: problem, approach, files, checkbox acceptance criteria.

03 · Build /ce-work

Take the plan, break it into tasks, implement, test, check off criteria. Context blows up? New session, point it at the plan, continue.

Hack 1 — The moment you have an idea, make a plan.md

Still rule number one. A crazy product idea → /ce-plan. A GitHub issue → copy the URL, paste, /ce-plan. A terminal error → screenshot with Cmd+Shift+4, paste with Ctrl+V, /ce-plan fix this. Claude Code reads images, so screenshots, mockups and Slack threads are all valid starting points. When the idea is still fuzzy and he doesn't know what he wants yet, he starts with /ce-brainstorm first, then /ce-plan once it's sharp.SRC 1

Under the hood, /ce-plan isn't sequential. It fans out agents in parallel — one reads the codebase for patterns and conventions, one mines your past solutions/ for lessons, others research external docs — then consolidates into a plan grounded in your repo and history, not generic advice. He frames the inversion plainly: traditional dev is 80% coding, 20% planning; this flips it. The thinking goes in the plan; execution is mechanical.SRC 1·2

The plan is the checkpoint that survives everything. If a session dies, start a new one, point it at the plan.md, and pick up where you left off. State lives in a file, not in fragile chat history.

Hack 2 — Don't read the plan New in June

The counterintuitive twist: he always makes the plan and almost never reads it. "Plans are for agents, you silly human." Forcing a plan to exist is what stops agents from being lazy — it makes them research, commit to an approach, write acceptance criteria, and actually hit them. The plan is the leash. He skims the title, runs /ce-work, and if he has a question he asks inline — "TLDR?", "eli5 this plan," "wait, why this approach?" — rather than reading 300 lines of markdown.SRC 1

Make the plan. Trust the plan. Don't read the plan.

Hack 3 — For deep non-code work, make a "plan for the plan" New in June

The biggest lesson since March: /ce-plan and /ce-work aren't just for code — it has a universal planning mode built in. The trick for serious knowledge work is to make the first plan a plan for how the work will be done. His example: after meeting GV's Michael Margolis, he handed Claude the man's book (PDF) plus a two-hour Granola transcript and said, roughly: don't write the document now — first plan how you'll read the book, mine the transcript, and produce a great document. It spent 45 minutes building an epic plan.SRC 1

The single best trick for making an LLM not lazy: ask it to plan how it will produce the deliverable, then execute that plan — and it does the deep version every time. Strategy docs, specs, competitive analysis and board updates all run through the same loop.

Inputs & Signal

Hack 4 — Get voice-pilled

Voice-to-LLM is different from voice-to-anything-else: the transcription doesn't need to be perfect because the listener understands context and guesses what the mic got wrong. You can mumble, trail off, restart. His setup: on Mac, Monologue (from Every) or Wispr Flow piping speech into whatever app is focused, plus a gooseneck mic; on phone, just Apple's built-in dictation — good enough because an LLM is on the other end. He famously dictated part of an article from his Tesla on Full Self-Driving while dropping off his kids.SRC 1·2

One honest admission: voice is great when he's alone but awkward in a shared office — he won't whisper into a mic around coworkers. A desk in an open room is still the weak spot in the whole workflow.SRC 1

Hack 12 — Be the human signal New in June

The mindset shift that took longest: when you run six agents, your job is not to do the work — it's to be the signal. Agents supply volume; you supply taste, direction, and the react-and-redirect loop. You look at what came back and say "option two is closer but use option one's language," "address the biggest risk," "this paragraph is too long," and they move.SRC 1

Be the taste. Let them be the hands.— Matt Van Horn, "Every Agentic Engineering Hack I Know"
The Terminal Setup

Hacks 5–6 — Lots of tabs, each opening straight into an agent

His actual day: four to six cmux tabs, each a separate session — one writing a plan, one building from another plan, one running /last30days, one fixing a bug found while testing. While one researches, he switches to another and builds; by the time he cycles back, the first is done. (He moved off a Ghostty-purist setup because he kept losing notifications.) And a new tab opens directly into Claude Code, not a shell — when a session costs one keystroke, you start far more of them. He also skips folders: the agent can find your project.SRC 1

Hack 8 — Dangerously skip permissions (and yes, he means it)

With six sessions you can't approve every action. The key flag is skipDangerousModePermissionPrompt: true — without it Claude re-asks every session (toggle with Shift+Tab). His stance: "It's my computer. GitHub is there if I break everything." A Stop sound hook is non-negotiable at six sessions, so you hear which one finished. Codex has the same YOLO mode via approval_policy = "never" / sandbox_mode = "danger-full-access".SRC 1·2

// ~/.claude/settings.json { "permissions": { "allow": ["WebSearch","WebFetch","Bash","Read","Write", "Edit","Glob","Grep","Task","TodoWrite"], "defaultMode": "bypassPermissions" }, "skipDangerousModePermissionPrompt": true, "hooks": { "Stop": [ { "hooks": [ { "type": "command", "command": "afplay /System/Library/Sounds/Blow.aiff" } ] } ] } }
A real caveat: this flips the risk envelope from "approve every step" to "do first, ask later." When he set up a friend's Claude Code, the AI actively tried to talk the friend out of enabling it. Great for speed; treat the environment with care.SRC 1

Hack 9 — Run most builds through Codex, without opening Codex

Claude plans, Codex builds, and he rarely leaves his Claude session. Three ways to hand work over: the Codex IDE extension, /ce-work --codex from inside Compound Engineering, or appending codex to a Printing Press prompt. He runs both engines at extra-high reasoning (Codex with fast mode on; Claude with fast mode off, since it bills per token on top of the Max plan). Two $200/month plans side by side is effectively a second engine — push big parallel builds to Codex, keep Claude on planning and taste.SRC 1·2

Hack 19 — Laptop & power

Six Claude sessions plus Codex wreck hardware: his new M5 Max with 64GB RAM still drains in as little as an hour. So he disables sleep (sudo pmset -a disablesleep 1), carries an Anker battery brick everywhere, and keeps a charger in the Tesla.SRC 1

Remote & Autonomous

Hack 7 — Reach every session, and give Claude an email

Two settings make every session reachable. Always-on remote control ("remoteControlAtStartup": true) means every window shows up in the Claude mobile app — start a run at your desk, pick it up live on your phone. And Claude Code can have its own inbox via AgentMail: email the address and a fresh session opens on whatever's in the subject, body and attachments. A bug at dinner becomes a running session before you're back at a screen. He open-sourced the whole thing (an allowlisted daemon that drops anything failing DKIM/SPF).SRC 1

Hack 15 — Work from anywhere: the Mac mini

A home Mac mini is the durable place his sessions live. Over bad Wi-Fi he uses Mosh (plain SSH makes Claude Code crawl, every keystroke waiting on the round trip). On planes he SSHes in inside a tmux session, so the work runs on the mini, not the laptop — Wi-Fi drops over the Atlantic, he reconnects, and it's exactly where he left it. He runs Hermes and OpenClaw for autonomous remote work, and Agent Cookie keeps cookies and .env files in sync between machines.SRC 1·2

The deeper point of "remote" isn't sending commands from your phone — it's that the session needs somewhere durable to live. Otherwise remote work is just remote restarting.
Beyond Code

Hacks 11 & 14 — Meetings and notes become the agent's memory

He records meetings with Granola and drops the raw transcript into /ce-plan — never summarizing first. One ~90-minute candidate lunch (sushi tangents and all) one-shotted into a product proposal, because Claude cross-referenced the transcript against the actual codebase and every prior strategy plan. The candidate now works there full-time. Since March, the Printing Press Granola CLI pulls any meeting as structured data and searches across every meeting he's ever had.SRC 1·2

He also points the agent at his whole "brain" as a personal knowledge base: Bear (via its CLI — a decade of notes), Obsidian, gbrain, and supermemory. Pick a notes tool with a CLI or API, point the agent at it, and your own knowledge compounds across sessions.SRC 1

Hacks 13 & 16 — Video, and sharing plans with humans New in June

HyperFrames lets him build video as HTML, so an agent writes it — each clip is a folder with a script.md, rendered to MP4. Same loop as code, output is just an MP4 instead of a PR. And Proof (from Every) closed the last gap: a plan.md is useless to hand a non-terminal colleague, so he drops it into Proof, sends a link, and comments flow back into the loop — human-in-the-loop review without pasting markdown into Slack.SRC 1

Hack 20 — Printing Press: CLIs that run real life New in June

The one hack that leaves the terminal. Printing Press generates agent-native CLIs that wrap real-world services, and Agent Cookie hands a CLI your real browser session so it acts as you — no passwords to paste. A real afternoon: "preheat the car to 72" (Tesla CLI), "add Corona to Costco on Instacart," a session that watched a game and pinged only when it got close, and pulling Alaska Airlines fares into /ce-plan for a booking strategy — from a soccer field.SRC 1

Not "AI writes my code." Agentic engineering does the errands, watches the game, warms the car, and books the trip — while you're doing something else.
Compounding

Hack 17 — Write your own skills

The real level-up isn't using agents — it's teaching them tricks that stick. Anything he does more than twice becomes a reusable skill. He doesn't write them from scratch: he points the agent at a skill that already works and says "look at the Compound Engineering skill and help me make one like this for [X]." It learns the structure and scaffolds his. last30days began this way; Printing Press is "a whole factory for generating agent-native CLIs."SRC 1

Write the skill once. Every session after is faster. That's the compounding part of Compound Engineering.

Hack 18 — Contribute to the open source you love

The same plan-first loop ships everyone else's projects too — hundreds of merged PRs, "not drive-by typo fixes," on tools he uses daily. He's landed near the top of several contributor lists. But he's emphatic that the merged PRs aren't the prize; the people are. He jumps into project Discords, meets maintainers, and even hired an engineer he met this way.SRC 1·2

Reflections

Hack 21 — The honest part: "AI psychosis" New in June

The section that sets the article apart. Agents were supposed to do the work for us; instead, every friend he has is working harder than ever. He frames it bluntly as addiction — "building with agents is the greatest video game ever made, and the loop is that good." He worries about friends so lit up by being able to build anything that they do nothing else, then launch to no users. His advice isn't "touch grass" as a cure-all: take breaks, talk to your loved ones, ask whether anyone actually wants the thing you're making — and if the honest answer is that it's just a tool for you, that's fine too.SRC 1

The trap isn't the empty launch — it's vanishing into the build and losing the people around you. (The March article's version of this was lighter: "My wife is mad at me… Sorry, sweetie.")SRC 1·2

The takeaway

Neither article is really about an IDE or a single tool. It's that voice input, a durable plan.md, parallel sessions and a growing fleet of CLIs compose into one repeatable operating model — and that same model now powers code, strategy, writing, open source, and Disney trips alike. Both articles were, fittingly, written this way: dictated into the terminal, rewritten on command, reviewed in Proof.SRC 1·2

Research, plan, build. No IDE worship, no vibe-coding for its own sake — talk, produce a plan that survives, and be the signal while agents do the hands-on work. From a desk, a couch, a car, or a soccer field.
